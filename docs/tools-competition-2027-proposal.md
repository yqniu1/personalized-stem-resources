## Proposal Abstract

*2,920 / 3,000 characters, including spaces and paragraph breaks.*

Teachers can identify a learning experience their students need, yet creating a custom interactive STEM resource can require technical and instructional-design expertise they cannot readily access. Finding and adapting existing materials also takes time. We propose an AI-powered authoring tool that helps K-12 teachers create personalized teaching resources through conversation, with an initial focus on interactive STEM materials.

A teacher describes a curriculum goal, provides relevant lesson material, and explains student needs and classroom constraints. The tool proposes an activity, builds a preview, and revises it through dialogue. Teachers retain control over the final resource. Behind this experience, agentic workflows combine prebuilt interactive tools with a knowledge source on learning-design patterns. Patterns such as coherence, scaffolding, and explanatory feedback will guide activity design and review. The tool will explain how learner actions support the lesson goal and flag issues for teacher review. We will test whether this combined approach helps teachers produce usable, curriculum-aligned resources with less assistance than a general-purpose AI workflow.

The starting point is a concrete feasibility example. I used conversational AI workflows and a rudimentary Learnability Patterns knowledge source to create an initial lily-dissection application for a teacher's need in under four hours. The Lily module has one user, and three teachers are providing feedback on the broader work. The current Lily resource supports a Grade 4 OpenSciEd lesson on flower structures and pollination, with interactive exploration and offline distribution. This demonstrates a development approach we can now make accessible to teachers themselves.

Catalyst support would bridge that transition: build a teacher-facing authoring experience, strengthen the pattern knowledge source and quality checks, and conduct a structured pilot. We would begin with a small set of reusable STEM activity types, invite the three feedback teachers to help refine the prototype, and recruit a broader pilot group once teachers can complete authoring tasks independently. Recruitment would seek teachers in schools with limited access to custom instructional-development support. Lightweight exports, keyboard controls, and a 2-D option where appropriate would be design priorities.

We aim to reduce total preparation effort while improving the fit between a resource, its curriculum goal, and its classroom context. Evaluation will measure creation and revision time, developer assistance, independent ratings of scientific accuracy and alignment, and classroom usability. Student explanations and work on targeted concepts will inform design improvements. We will share an evaluation rubric and protocol so the work contributes to research on AI-assisted instructional design as well as a practical teacher tool.

## Learning Engineering

*872 / 1,000 characters, including spaces and paragraph breaks.*

We will link each resource's curriculum goal, selected patterns, versions, and teacher revisions to quality ratings and, in consenting pilot classrooms, student responses to targeted concept tasks. These data will help identify usability problems and generate hypotheses about design and learning. Offline resources would support teacher-mediated collection or export.

Recurring reviews will inform improvements to patterns, components, and workflows. We will seek an external learning researcher to develop measures and compare the combined approach with a general-purpose AI baseline on matched tasks. The pilot will establish feasibility; later controlled studies would test learning effects and the contribution of particular patterns. We will publish an evaluation rubric, protocol, and aggregate findings, with raw student data excluded from default public outputs.

## Path to Scale

*957 / 1,000 characters, including spaces and paragraph breaks.*

Scale depends on making authoring repeatable without developer assistance. We will start with a few STEM activity types, invite our three feedback teachers to help refine the prototype, and recruit a broader pilot once teachers can independently create and revise usable resources. Reusable components and pattern-guided workflows can then support additional lessons and subjects without rebuilding every interaction.

Teachers will be able to adapt and share resources, with lightweight browser exports designed for ordinary school devices and offline classroom use where feasible. The existing Lily example demonstrates this distribution approach. AI inference, asset creation, hosting, and support remain real costs; we will measure cost per usable resource, including failed attempts and revisions. Pilot access will be free. A sustainable pricing and access model will be developed from those findings, with attention to schools with limited resources.
